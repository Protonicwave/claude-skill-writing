# Decision register

Recommendations, options appraisals, design choices and requests for a decision, whether they go as an email, a note to a group or a section of a report.

Read with this file: `registers/message.md` for an email or message; `registers/report.md` for a section of a report or plan; `clarity.md` for likelihood words.

## The target

A reader who has not seen the notes can say yes or no after one reading, knowing what they agree to and what it costs. The writer has done the weighing; the reader checks it.

## Rules

- **The recommendation comes first.** The first two sentences give the choice and the reason that decides it, so a reader who stops there knows what to approve. Background follows only as far as the reader needs to agree. The exception is a reader who asked for the options without a view: set them out and name the criterion that would choose.
- **Test every option against the hard limits before weighing it.** List each limit the inputs set: budget, allocation, deadline, a rule in a handbook or brief, who must approve what above which amount. Do the arithmetic for each option ("eight runs at 30 hours is 240 hours; the quota leaves 150"). An option that breaks a limit is ruled out by naming the limit, however good it is otherwise, because the reader cannot carry it out. If the limit could be lifted, asking to lift it becomes the decision.
- **Compare on the two or three criteria that decide this choice**, with figures from the notes, not on generic pros and cons. Say what each surviving option gives up. A table only when three or more options are compared on three or more criteria.
- **Answer every question already on the table.** A question the reader raised, or an option a teammate favours, gets a sentence saying what the recommendation does with it: answered, answered in a smaller form, or deferred to a named date. Left out, it reads as missed.
- **Give the recommended option's main cost or risk** and how it will be contained. A recommendation with no downside reads as unexamined.
- **Say what would change the recommendation**: the result or event that would make another option better ("if the C++ version fails its energy check by week 3, we switch to Python"). A disagreement becomes a condition someone can check.
- **A likelihood is a number, or a word on one scale.** Give the probability where the notes support one; otherwise use the yardstick in `clarity.md` within its ranges. Never "may" or "could" for a risk the decision rests on.
- **End on the decision asked for.** The last sentence is one direct question the reader can answer in one reply, naming what is needed: an approval with its amount, a choice, a date. Nothing follows it, not a fallback plan or "let me know". Several decisions go in one numbered question. A document for a group with no single approver ends on what the group must decide and by when.
- **Costs and odds stay the student's.** Never invent a time, cost or probability to complete the comparison: mark it `[DATA NEEDED: what]`. Totals, shortfalls and run times derived from the notes are the analysis: show the working once.

## Length

Unless one is set: a recommendation email under 250 words, an options appraisal under 400. Cut background before any option's cost, and log the length chosen.

A decision under 400 words gets three yes/no tests, no more, and a judgement log of the three choices the student is likeliest to reverse: a short text has few choices worth reversing, and each extra line is read before the decision.

## Example

Buried:

> I've been thinking about how best to use the remaining beam time. There are a few options, each with advantages. More statistics on the energy scan would strengthen chapter 3, while the detector test could also be useful. Let me know what you think.

Decided:

> I recommend we spend the last four shifts on the detector test rather than the energy scan: the scan needs six shifts and we have four. The test also answers the calibration question you raised, since it uses the same source. The main risk is that the detector fails its first check, which would cost one shift before we return to the scan. Can you approve the four shifts for the detector test?
