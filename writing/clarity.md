# Clarity without losing precision

The reader is intelligent and outside the field: a first-year physics student for the body of a report, an educated non-specialist for a lay summary. Precision wins over simplicity. Removing a technical term is not explaining it.

Sources: Gopen and Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (American Scientist, 1990); Knuth, Larrabee and Roberts, "Mathematical Writing" (Stanford, 1987); Mermin, "Writing Physics" (Cornell, 1999); Durham University Physics report guidance; IOP Publishing language guidance.

## Introducing a term

1. Decide whether the reader needs the name at all. If the term appears once, describe the thing and skip the name.
2. Put the new term at the end of a sentence whose start holds something the reader already has: "Each spin is set by one pixel of the modulator, and the pattern of all the pixels encodes a trial solution called the *spin configuration*."
3. Define it two ways: what it is physically or operationally, then the formula or an example. "The dephasing time is how long a superposition keeps a definite phase; formally, the decay constant of the off-diagonal density-matrix element."
4. From then on, use exactly that term, in exactly that form, for that concept and nothing else. Spell out an abbreviation once, then use it consistently.
5. Where an everyday word has a different technical meaning (work, power, error, uncertainty, significant, observe, random, memory, spin), say once which sense is meant, or choose a word that does not mislead. Reserve "significant" for statistical significance with a stated test.
6. Keep a running list of fixed terms and symbols while writing. Same symbol, same font, same meaning throughout.

Show before you name when the concept is unfamiliar: the instance first (this run, this output, this number), the general term second.

## Explaining an equation

1. Before the equation, say what question it answers: "The energy the machine minimises depends on every pair of spins."
2. Display it as part of the sentence, punctuated and numbered.
3. After it, define every new symbol in words with its units: "where *k* is the thermal conductivity of the rod (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹)". Write "where *g* is the gradient", never "where *g* = gradient".
4. Then read it physically: what grows with what, what limit it reduces to, what the shape of the dependence means.
5. Apply the blah test: replace the equation with "blah" and the paragraph must still make sense. A reader who skips symbols still gets the claim.
6. Refer back by name and number: "the Ising energy (2)". An equation with no nameable role may be a step the reader does not need.
7. Never start a sentence with a symbol. Separate adjacent formulas with words. No logical symbols (therefore, implies, for all) in prose.

Typesetting: italic variables, upright numbers, functions and units, a space between number and unit, uncertainties as (1.42 ± 0.01) × 10⁻¹ m.

## Ordering information

1. Context before content. Each paragraph opens with what the reader holds and ends with the new point.
2. Old to new within each sentence. The new item at the end of one sentence becomes the topic at the start of the next, which gives the reader a chain to follow.
3. Subject, then verb, quickly. Move long qualifiers after the verb or into their own sentence. A 20-word interruption between subject and verb forces a reread.
4. One main claim per sentence where the material is new to the reader. If two items compete for the stress position at the end, split the sentence.
5. Concrete before abstract: the instance, then the generalisation.
6. Results as numbers with uncertainty and a comparison, so the reader judges without being told how to feel.

## Analogies

An analogy helps when the same relation holds in both things (a curved mirror really is many small flat mirrors), when the reader knows the analogue well, and when it is used for one relation and then dropped. It misleads when readers carry over features that do not map (electron orbits, a spacetime fabric), when it replaces the explanation instead of preparing for it, or when it is a stock figure of speech.

The rule for this skill:

- Use an analogy only if it is exact, and only if it takes fewer words than the literal explanation.
- Name the target, give the analogy, say which feature maps, and state at least one place it fails.
- If you cannot state where it fails, do not use it.
- Return to the precise statement immediately afterwards.

## Precision

- Replace vague qualifiers with quantities: "high frequency" becomes "above 10 kHz, well above the 50 Hz mains"; "reliable" says for what and to what tolerance.
- Keep "measured" and "calculated" distinct. Name the method ("least-squares fit"), not the software function.
- Hedges carry information or go.
- Plain verbs over stiff ones: used, not utilised; tried, not attempted; wrote, not authored.

## Conflicts between the sources, resolved

| Question | Resolution |
|---|---|
| Passive or active? | Passive where the actor is irrelevant and the object is the topic (most method steps); active everywhere else. |
| "I" or "we"? | Follow the department's rule for assessed work. Otherwise "we" means author and reader together, and "I" is for the author's own judgement in single-author work. |
| Jargon? | Keep the term, define it once (above). |
| Repetition? | Vary sentence structure; never vary the name of a concept. |
| Sentence length? | Short by default, with length driven by content. |
