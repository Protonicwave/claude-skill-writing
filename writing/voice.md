# Tone on request

A request for a tone ("warmer", "more formal", "less blunt", "punchier") names a direction, not a destination. Treat tone as five dials, each with a register default and a set of concrete things it moves. Each dial has four settings: none, low, medium and high. A request moves the dials its words name, by one setting unless it says "much" or "very", and leaves the others where the register or the student's own text set them. Some words name two dials: "punchier" moves concision and directness up together.

When the student's own text is the starting point, its level on each dial is the base, not the register default. A tone change to the student's text changes tone and nothing else.

## What no tone changes

Every fact, number, date, name and deadline. Every request, decision and consequence the text carries. The gap markers. The size of every hedge, because confidence follows the evidence. And the rules in `SKILL.md`: UK English, no dashes as punctuation, no chatbot residue, no emoji. If a tone cannot be reached without breaking one of these, keep the rule and say what the tone could not do.

## The dials

### Formality

What it moves: contractions; greeting and sign-off; first names or titles; everyday or Latinate words (get, use, check against obtain, utilise, verify); how complete each sentence is.

Up: "more formal", "professional", "for the department", "for the committee". Down: "more casual", "relaxed", "less stiff", "for the group chat".

High is not stiff: no "Please find attached", no "I am writing to inform you", none of the stock phrases in `tells.md`. Low is not slang: no text-speak, no emoji, nothing the student would not say aloud to the reader.

### Warmth

What it moves: whether the reader is addressed by name; "we" for shared work and shared problems; thanks for a specific thing the reader did; acknowledgement of the reader's own constraint ("I know marking week is busy"); an offer of help that is real and specific.

Up: "warmer", "friendlier", "kinder", "softer", "less harsh". Down: "more neutral", "businesslike", "just the facts".

Warmth is specific or it is filler. "Hope everyone is well", a row of exclamation marks, praise with no object and "no worries at all!" add no warmth; they read as a template. The test: every warm sentence names something true about this reader or this situation.

### Directness

What it moves: where the request or the point sits (first line, or after the context); a bare imperative, a question, or a statement of what is needed; whether the text says who does what.

Up: "more direct", "get to the point", "punchier", "firmer", "clearer about what I need". Down: "gentler", "more diplomatic", "less pushy".

Lower directness changes the form of the request, never whether it is there. "The plotting script must be fixed" can become "Could we get the plotting script fixed?", but the deadline and the consequence stay as clear as before. Keep the request in the first two sentences whatever the setting: lower directness adds context before it, not a paragraph of it.

"Less blunt" usually means less blame and fewer bare imperatives, not a vaguer request. Blame sits in the subject of the sentence: "you broke the build", "whoever pushed last", "someone needs to stop" all point at a person everyone can identify. Make the problem the subject ("the build fails at the plotting step") and the fix shared ("could we"), and drop any reproach ("I've already said this twice").

### Confidence

What it moves: hedges that carry no information; whether results and judgements are stated flatly; first person for the writer's own judgement ("I think the fit fails because"); whether the student's achievements are stated or apologised for.

Up: "more confident", "more assertive", "sell myself", "stop underselling it". Down: "more modest", "more tentative", "less sure of itself".

Confidence is capped by the evidence in both directions. Up removes empty hedges and says plainly what the evidence shows; it never states more than the evidence holds and never adds adjectives about the writer ("highly skilled", "passionate"). Down states uncertainty where it exists and with its size; it never invents doubt about a result the data settle.

### Concision

What it moves: words per point. Cut in this order: padding and stock phrases, context the reader already has, repeated points, examples beyond the first, then subordinate detail. Expand in the reverse order, explaining what is already there.

Up: "shorter", "tighter", "punchier", "cut it down", a word limit. Down: "fuller", "more detail", "expand", "flesh it out".

Never cut a fact the reader needs to act or to judge the claim. Never expand with a new claim, number or source: more detail means more of the student's own material, and where there is none, a `[DATA NEEDED: what]` marker or a question to the student. "Punchier" never means one-sentence paragraphs.

## Defaults by register

| Register | Formality | Warmth | Directness | Confidence | Concision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Report | high | low | high | high | high |
| Lab book | medium | none | high | medium | high |
| Essay | high | low | high | high | medium |
| Explainer | medium | medium | medium | medium | medium |
| Message | medium | medium | high | medium | high |
| Application | high | medium | high | high | high |
| Post | medium | medium | high | medium | high |

Confidence at any setting stays within what the evidence holds, as its dial says. A message's formality moves one setting up for someone the student has not written to before or for a committee, and one down for a teammate. A register's own file and a rubric outrank this table. A request outranks both, within the limits above.

## Conflicting requests

Some requests pull a dial both ways ("shorter but with more detail on the method"). Usually they mean different parts of the text: cut elsewhere and keep the method. When they cannot be reconciled within a word limit, ask one question. When a requested tone would break a rule above (more confident than the data allow, warmer by adding an invented compliment), apply the rest and say in the delivery note which part was held back and why.

## Example

Blunt:

> The results table is wrong again. Someone needs to rerun the fits with the corrected calibration before Thursday, I'm not doing it a third time.

Warmer and less blunt, with the request, the deadline and the refusal kept:

> The results table still has the old calibration in it, which is easy to miss because both files have the same name. Could someone rerun the fits with the corrected file before Thursday? I've done the last two runs, so it would help if someone else took this one.

The request and the deadline are in the second sentence. The reproach is gone: the problem is the subject, and the writer's refusal to run the fits a third time stays, stated as a reason rather than a complaint. The warmth is a specific explanation, not praise or an offer of help the original never made. The explanation here is invented for the example's shape; in real text it comes from the student, or the sentence goes.
