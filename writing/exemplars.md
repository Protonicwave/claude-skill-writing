# Exemplars

Short passages from writers admired for clarity, all published before November 2022 and so certainly human. Each was fetched from the URL given and checked word for word. They are here to calibrate the voice, not to be copied: take the move, never the words. Quotations keep their original spelling.

If the student supplies writing of their own, that outranks everything in this file.

## Open on the specific problem

> I propose to consider the question, "Can machines think?" This should begin with definitions of the meaning of the terms "machine" and "think."

Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", Mind 59, 433 (1950). https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf

The first sentence is the question. No preamble about the importance of computing.

Counter-example, from an otherwise outstanding 2021 Edinburgh dissertation: "The current decade is going to be one of the most exciting times in the history of Computer Science." It could open a thousand reports. That dissertation's real opening was its second paragraph, which starts with RSA.

## State the whole problem early, and draw its boundary

> The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point. Frequently the messages have meaning; that is they refer to or are correlated according to some system with certain physical or conceptual entities. These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem.

Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", Bell System Technical Journal 27 (1948). https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf

A reader who stops here knows what the paper does and what it will not attempt. The boundary is one flat sentence.

> That's an interesting subject, but is not what I want to talk about. I want to talk about the possibility that there is to be an exact simulation, that the computer will do exactly the same as nature.

Feynman, "Simulating Physics with Computers", Int. J. Theor. Phys. 21, 467 (1982). https://s2.smu.edu/~mitch/class/5395/papers/feynman-quantum-1981.pdf

Naming the excluded case makes the included one precise.

## Keep the exact term and gloss it in the same sentence

> Computer scientists use the word "graph" to refer to a network of nodes with edges connecting some of the nodes. The graph isomorphism question simply asks when two graphs are really the same graph in disguise because there's a one-to-one correspondence (an "isomorphism") between their nodes that preserves the ways the nodes are connected.

Klarreich, "Landmark Algorithm Breaks 30-Year Impasse", Quanta Magazine (2015). https://www.quantamagazine.org/algorithm-solves-graph-isomorphism-in-record-time-20151214/

The plain gloss ("the same graph in disguise") comes before the formal name, which then rides in brackets. The exact term is never diluted.

> The sustain, how long a note sounds, is characteristic of a guitar.

Davis, Drewes, Lomax and Norman, Journal of Physics Special Topics 19 (Leicester, 2020). https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/pst/article/view/3678

A student paper. An appositive defines the key term in the first sentence, so a non-musician is not lost by line one.

> Locality is the notion that particles can interact only from adjoining positions in space and time. And unitarity holds that the probabilities of all possible outcomes of a quantum mechanical interaction must add up to one.

Wolchover, "A Jewel at the Heart of Quantum Physics", Quanta Magazine (2013). https://www.quantamagazine.org/physicists-discover-geometry-underlying-particle-physics-20130917/

The term is the subject; the definition is the predicate. Two terms, two sentences.

## Define by the nearest neighbour, then the difference

> The technique is essentially an execution trace, though one intended to help with understanding the program, not debugging it.

Wasey, "Bringing The Stupid Computer To Life", MInf report (Edinburgh, 2021, Outstanding). https://project-archive.inf.ed.ac.uk/ug4/20212179/ug4_proj.pdf

A student relates the course's informal term to the field's formal one and says how it differs. The worked trace came first; this sentence names it afterwards.

## Turn claims into numbers the reader can check

> For graphs with just 10 nodes, for instance, there are already more than 3 million possible matchings to check. And for graphs with 100 nodes, the number of possible matchings far exceeds the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe.

Klarreich (2015), as above.

> The head of a pin is a sixteenth of an inch across. If you magnify it by 25,000 diameters, the area of the head of the pin is then equal to the area of all the pages of the Encyclopaedia Brittanica.

Feynman, "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom" (1959). https://www.zyvex.com/nanotech/feynman.html

The bold claim arrives as arithmetic, so the reader checks it instead of trusting it.

## Let length follow the content

> Let me start not logically, but psychologically. I find that the major objection is that people think great science is done by luck. It's all a matter of luck. Well, consider Einstein. Note how many different things he did that were good. Was it all luck? Wasn't it a little too repetitive?

Hamming, "You and Your Research" (1986). https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html

Sentences of 7, 16, 6, 3, 10, 4 and 6 words. The questions do the arguing. This is a spoken talk: take the rhythm, not the informality.

Across twelve admired writers, sentence length averages 18 to 29 words with a spread (standard deviation) of 10 to 17 words. The most uniform text in the set, at 9.5, is also the one that reads flattest.

## Separate fact from conjecture, and hedge once

> Provided it is made clear which are proved facts and which are conjectures, no harm can result. Conjectures are of great importance since they suggest useful lines of research.

Turing (1950), as above.

> Obviously there are plenty of things we don't understand. We don't know how to quantize gravity, or what the dark matter is, or what breaks electroweak symmetry. But we don't need to know any of those things to account for the world that is immediately apparent to us.

Carroll, blog post, 23 September 2010. https://preposterousuniverse.com/blog/2010/09/23/the-laws-underlying-the-physics-of-everyday-life-are-completely-understood/

The concession is specific (three named unknowns), so the claim that follows is believable. No "somewhat", no "arguably".

## Report what went wrong, with numbers

> As this project featured rather complex kernels (up to 1706 lines for JaTAM classification, which is at least 17- times larger than typical kernel sizes according to Langdon (Langdon, 2011)), virtually all of the pitfalls reported by Langdon were encountered. Some of these resulted in intraceable bugs and lead three times to a complete redesign of our source code methodology.

Schroeder de Witt, "Biological Evolution and Genetic Algorithms", MPhys thesis (Oxford, 2012, Tessella Prize). https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15311

A physics MPhys with a computing contribution, the closest match to this degree. The difficulty is quantified and sourced, and it becomes part of the method. The slips ("lead" for "led", "intraceable") show unretouched student prose; keep the honesty, not the slips.

> Details that could throw doubt on your interpretation must be given, if you know them.

Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science" (1974). https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.htm

What a results and discussion section owes the reader, in fifteen words.

## Name the reader's objection and answer it

> You might question the "once and for all" part of that formulation, but it's solid. Of course revolutions can always happen, but there's every reason to believe that our current understanding is complete within the everyday realm.

Carroll (2010), as above.

## Use one analogy that fits, and do not stretch it

> We are in a position similar to that of a man who was provided with a bunch of keys and who, having to open several doors in succession, always hit on the right key on the first or second trial. He became skeptical concerning the uniqueness of the coordination between keys and doors.

Wigner, "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences", Commun. Pure Appl. Math. 13, 1 (1960). https://webhomes.maths.ed.ac.uk/~v1ranick/papers/wigner.pdf

One structural analogy, used once, for one relation.

## "I" and "we"

> It is not just pompous to make "we" the authorial voice in a single author paper. It deprives you of an opportunity to distinguish gracefully between when you're speaking for yourself and when you have in mind both yourself and your reader.

Mermin, "Writing Physics" (1999). https://mermin.lassp.cornell.edu/KnightLecture.html

Follow the department's rule for assessed work; where it is silent, this is the distinction to keep.

## End on the limitation or the open problem

> While we ignored some more complex aspects of the guitar, such as the neck and shape of the guitar, this model does qualitatively reproduce the basic physical characteristics of solid body guitars.

Davis et al. (2020), as above.

> We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done.

Turing (1950), as above. The last sentence of the paper.

## What these writers refuse to do

- Open with the importance of the field before the reader knows the problem.
- Introduce a term without a gloss or an example within a sentence or two.
- Swap synonyms for a technical term.
- Hedge every sentence.
- Hide failures, partial coverage or why a result fell short.
- Decorate. Interest comes from the problem itself.
